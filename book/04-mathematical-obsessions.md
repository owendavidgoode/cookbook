## The Constants as Bridges

**Pi** dominates with 153 post titles.

The fascination spans seventeen years, from computational tricks ("Computing pi with bc") to surprising appearances ("The coupon collector problem and π").

Why pi? Not because of circles. Pi appears in the probability of a random walk returning to the origin. It appears in the distribution of prime numbers. It appears in the gamma function. Pi is a bridge: it connects geometry to probability to number theory. When Cook writes about pi, he is rarely writing about circumference; he is writing about connection.

**Primes** (103 post titles) connect the ancient to the urgent.

The blog traces the arc from Euclid ("multiply all primes together and add 1") to RSA encryption. Primes are the atoms of arithmetic, but they are also the foundation of digital security. Cook's treatment captures this dual nature: playful explorations of "Limerick primes" sit alongside serious discussions of primality testing for cryptography.

**Randomness** (95 post titles) connects the abstract to the concrete.

How do you generate points in Colorado? How do you simulate a parachute trial? Randomness is the tool for turning uncertainty into data. The blog's posts on Monte Carlo methods and quasi-random sequences are practical guides for using chaos to solve order.

## The Named Functions

A special function is just a function useful enough to deserve a name. Names emerge from use.

**The Gamma Function** (18 posts) is "the most important function not on a calculator."

It extends the factorial to non-integers. Because factorials appear in combinatorics, probability, and calculus, the gamma function appears in all of them too. It is the blog's favorite tool for normalizing distributions and solving integrals. It bridges discrete mathematics (counting) and continuous mathematics (calculus).

**Fourier Transforms** (45 posts tagged "Fourier analysis") decompose the world into frequencies.

The 2023 post "Fixed points of the Fourier transform" asks: which functions equal their own transform? The normal distribution does. The hyperbolic secant does. The question reveals a deep structure connecting signal processing to probability theory.

**Bessel Functions** (12 posts) bridge physics and geometry. They describe heat in cylinders and vibrations on drumheads. A physicist needs them as often as a carpenter needs a hammer.

The tag "Special functions" marks 140 posts. These are the working tools of the trade.

## The Web of Connection

The blog's obsession is not with the objects themselves, but with the web they form.

- Pi appears in the normal distribution (alongside *e*).
- Primes underlie the security of random number generators.
- Euler's formula links pi, *e*, and the imaginary unit.
- The gamma function normalizes the beta distribution, which connects to Bayesian statistics.

The posts trace these lines. A 2025 post on pi approximations notes a formula hinting at Heegner numbers. The blog doesn't just share the formula; it notes the hint.

This is what a mathematical bestiary reveals: an ecosystem. The functions that recur are the ones that connect domains.

The blog's engagement with Ramanujan (10 posts) captures this. Ramanujan found simple formulas connecting unrelated constants. His pi approximation gives 18 correct decimal places using only roots and fractions. Cook quotes Hardy calling one result "most beautiful," then admits: "If I understood it, I might say it's beautiful, but for now I can only say it's mysterious."

Humility before difficulty, combined with persistent engagement—that is the stance.

## Why These Objects?

A working mathematician returns to certain constants not for novelty, but for utility.

Pi solves problems about waves. Primes solve problems about encryption. The gamma function normalizes distributions. Each is a tool, sharpened over millennia.

The blog's treatment reflects this. Posts rarely introduce a constant from scratch. They encounter it in use—solving a problem, appearing in a formula, bridging two fields.

The reader learns pi not as "the ratio of circumference to diameter" but as "the thing that appears when you calculate the probability of a random walk returning to the origin."

That's what a mathematical imagination orbits: not the new, but the foundational. The numbers that keep appearing are the ones that keep being useful.

# Editorial Assessment: *You Can Just Do Things*

*Note: This assessment was written when the working title was "Methodical." The book has since been retitled "You Can Just Do Things: How to Become Internet Famous."*

## Snapshot Overview

**What's working:**
- Clear, confident voice that sustains across 24,000 words without sagging
- Strong conceptual premise: data-as-portrait, patterns-as-biography
- Excellent prologue—hooks quickly, positions the work, manages expectations
- Smart structure that moves from macro (architecture) to micro (obsessions) to synthetic (long view)
- Chapter 12 is a genuine strength—acknowledging limits earns the reader's trust
- The epilogue's transparency about AI-assisted creation is honest and adds meta-interest

**What isn't working yet:**
- Chapters 4–6 (Part II: The Obsessions) feel repetitive in structure and flatten into catalog mode
- Too many tables; the prose-to-data ratio needs rebalancing in several chapters
- The "so what" for secondary readers (non-John readers) is underdeveloped
- Several chapters land softly—they end with restatement rather than arrival
- The interviews chapter (10) is undercooked; it tells us what happened but not what it means
- Risk of monotony: nearly every chapter follows "question → data table → interpretation → restatement"

**One-sentence articulation of the book's promise:**
*This book treats a single person's seventeen years of public writing as a data set, then reads the patterns to reveal the shape of sustained intellectual practice.*

---

## Big-Picture Editorial Letter

### Concept and Positioning

The concept is sharp and unusual: a "data portrait" of a blog, offered as a gift to its author. This is not biography, not criticism, not tribute in the sentimental sense—it's something rarer. The framing works.

The risk is that the concept is *so* specific (one blog, one recipient) that secondary readers may struggle to find their foothold. The prologue does some of this work ("influence without fame, infrastructure without brand"), but the book could do more to signal why a non-Cook reader should care. What does this one case reveal about sustained practice, about the shape of intellectual work, about what it means to keep showing up?

The answer is implicit throughout, but it should be explicit somewhere early—perhaps in a revised prologue or a brief author's note. The pitch to the tertiary audience (data-viz enthusiasts, blogging historians, people curious about intellectual persistence) needs sharpening.

**Positioning suggestion:** This book sits in the neighborhood of *Range* by David Epstein (generalism), *The War of Art* by Steven Pressfield (creative practice), and *Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman* (intellectual personality)—but it's not like any of them. It's closest perhaps to *Daily Rituals* by Mason Currey: a study of *how* someone works, not *what* they produced. Lean into that.

### Reader and Transformation

The primary reader (John D. Cook) will presumably find this gratifying—a mirror held up to work he may not have seen from this angle. The transformation for him is recognition: "So that's what I've been doing."

The secondary reader (his existing audience) will find confirmation and detail—their sense that the blog is unusually valuable, now backed by data.

The tertiary reader (newcomers, data enthusiasts) needs more. What transformation does this book offer them? My suggestion: the transformation is *permission and method*. Permission to pursue sustained, quiet work rather than chasing virality. Method in seeing how patterns reveal character. The book should make this explicit.

### Structure and Pacing

The five-part structure is sound in theory:
- Part I (Architecture): macro view
- Part II (Obsessions): recurring subjects
- Part III (Bridge): math meets code
- Part IV (Unexpected): edges and interviews
- Part V (Long View): synthesis and limits

In practice, **Parts II and III are where the book sags**. Chapters 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8 follow nearly identical patterns: introduce a question, present a data table, interpret the data, offer a mild takeaway. The reader can predict the rhythm by Chapter 5. This is the "catalog" problem: interesting information presented without sufficient variation in approach.

**Structural prescription:**
1. **Combine Chapters 4 and 5** into a single "Constants and Functions" chapter—the material overlaps conceptually (recurring mathematical objects), and separating them creates redundancy.
2. **Recast Chapter 6** to focus more on *relationships* (how does a modern mathematician position himself relative to giants?) and less on tallying mentions. Currently it's the weakest chapter.
3. **Chapter 7 and 8** could be tightened—the PowerShell fossil is a strong beat, but it gets lost in repetitive structure.

Part IV is better—the "edges" frame gives life to Music, Typography, and Clinical Trials as *revelations* rather than mere categories. Chapter 10 (Interviews) is the structural anomaly: it's the only chapter about *other voices*, and it should feel different. Currently it feels like more catalog.

Part V lands well. Chapter 11 synthesizes without repeating. Chapter 12 is the book's quiet triumph—the acknowledgment of limits earns everything that came before.

### Voice and Tone

The voice is strong: measured, confident, slightly austere. It matches the subject. A few passages drift toward the academic ("The gamma function—the mathematical tool that extends factorials beyond whole numbers—appears in 18 posts, spanning the blog's entire history")—the parenthetical explanations occasionally feel obligatory rather than generous.

The tone is consistent throughout, which is good, but the emotional range is narrow. The book could use a few moments of wonder, humor, or even affection. The material permits it (the 5-word "Music recommendations" post is funny; the 2014 mystery is poignant). Don't oversell these moments, but don't suppress them either.

### Market and Comp Observations

This is a niche book by design. It has no obvious commercial category—it's not self-help, not biography, not "how to blog." The closest comps might be:
- *Daily Rituals* by Mason Currey (creative process as subject)
- *How to Take Smart Notes* by Sönke Ahrens (intellectual infrastructure)
- *Several Short Sentences About Writing* by Verlyn Klinkenborg (craft meditation)

The book will find its audience through John D. Cook's own channels first, then through word-of-mouth among the math/tech/data community. The constraint is clear: this is a gift-book first, a product second. The commercial framing should acknowledge that.

## Chapter-by-Chapter Notes

### Prologue: The Quiet Institution

**Purpose:** Hook the reader, establish the "quiet institution" thesis, manage expectations.

**What works:**
- Opening with "5,233 posts" is strong—concrete, immediate.
- The "climax species" metaphor is memorable.
- The "what this book is not" section is useful and honest.
- Final line ("This is the shape of that work") lands.

**What doesn't:**
- The middle section (paragraphs 3–5) slightly overextends the "influence without fame" point. Three examples of 2 AM searchers would be more powerful than the current abstraction.
- "The internet's climax species" metaphor is good but arrives without setup—a sentence earlier about succession ecology would anchor it.

**Specific suggestion:**
The paragraph beginning "What does it mean to do one thing for seventeen years?" is strong conceptually but soft rhythmically. Consider breaking it into shorter sentences for more punch.

**Before:**
> What does it mean to do one thing for seventeen years? The internet doesn't reward persistence. It rewards novelty, outrage, timing.

**After:**
> What does it mean to do one thing for seventeen years?
>
> The internet doesn't reward persistence. It rewards novelty. Outrage. Timing.

---

### Chapter 1: The Shape of Seventeen Years

**Purpose:** Establish scale, introduce the three-era framework, show the quantity/depth tradeoff.

**What works:**
- The year-by-year table is essential and well-used.
- The 2014 "quiet year" mystery is compelling.
- The pandemic non-event is well-observed ("For a blog that exists on the internet, written by a person who presumably lives in the world, the near absence of pandemic disruption suggests either remarkable discipline or a practice so deeply ingrained that external circumstances barely register").
- The "What Changed / What Didn't Change" structure at the end provides closure.

**What doesn't:**
- The opening is slightly slow—the first two paragraphs describe posts without hooking.
- The "Raw Numbers" section could be trimmed; the exact word count (1,823,200) matters less than the "18 novels" comparison.
- The pull quote ("Paying too much attention to what is 'practical' can be self-defeating") doesn't obviously connect to the chapter's argument.

**Specific suggestion:**
The ending falls into restatement: "5,233 posts. 1.8 million words. 6,531 days. One voice, present and accounted for. What does sustained intellectual output look like? This." This repeats the prologue's ending too closely. Find a different landing.

---

### Chapter 2: The Topography of Interest

**Purpose:** Map the blog by category, explore what the taxonomy reveals.

**What works:**
- "If the blog were a country, Math would be the territory west of the Mississippi" is a strong image.
- The "Uncategorized Mystery" section is well-framed—treating the escape from taxonomy as meaningful.
- The overlaps section (Math+Music, Clinical trials+Statistics) is genuinely illuminating.
- The closing ("The twenty-sixth category—Uncategorized—is the most honest one") is excellent.

**What doesn't:**
- The chapter is too long. Three data tables in close succession (full category breakdown, top ten Math tags, top five combinations) create catalog fatigue.
- The "Evolution of the Territory" section repeats information from Chapter 1 (Math percentage rising over time).
- The pull quote (Colin McLarty on cohomology) is too abstract for most readers.

**Specific suggestion:**
Cut the "Evolution of the Territory" section entirely—save this for Chapter 11 (Evolution), where it belongs. This chapter should stay spatial (the map), not temporal (the change).

---

### Chapter 3: The Rhythm of a Working Mind

**Purpose:** Analyze temporal patterns (day of week, hour of day, month) to reveal work habits.

**What works:**
- The "Tuesday is peak day" observation is genuinely interesting.
- The hourly analysis ("This is a morning person") is strong and specific.
- The flat monthly distribution ("No seasonal pattern") is a surprising finding, well-interpreted.
- The "Anti-Pattern" section at the end is smart: Cook says he has no schedule, but the data shows profound rhythm.

**What doesn't:**
- The connected scatterplot description (posts vs. length by year) feels like it belongs in Chapter 1, not here.
- The coefficient of variation discussion (monthly CV) is too technical for the non-statistician reader.
- The chapter ends with a restatement ("Seven o'clock in the morning, usually Tuesday, regardless of month: that's when the writing happens") rather than an arrival.

**Specific suggestion:**
The chapter should end on the "Anti-Pattern" insight—the gap between what Cook says (no schedule) and what the data shows (profound regularity). That's the interesting finding. The current ending buries it.

---

### Chapter 4: The Numbers That Keep Appearing

**Purpose:** Identify the mathematical constants (pi, primes, random, Fibonacci, e) that recur across seventeen years.

**What works:**
- The opening frame ("Some numbers earn names. Others earn attention.") is good.
- The first/last appearance dates for each constant are illuminating.
- The "Web of Connection" section (how constants relate to each other) adds value.

**What doesn't:**
- The structure is repetitive: Pi section, Primes section, Random section, Fibonacci section, Euler section—each follows the same pattern. The reader can predict the shape by the third section.
- The sections are too parallel in length and structure. They feel like encyclopedia entries, not narrative.
- The chapter promises "The Numbers That Keep Appearing" but only covers five items. It's a short list dressed as a major finding.

**Specific suggestion:**
Merge this chapter with Chapter 5 (Bestiary of Functions). Both cover "recurring mathematical objects"—the distinction between constants (pi, e) and functions (gamma, Bessel) is real but not significant enough to warrant separate chapters. A combined chapter would have more room to explore connections and less pressure to pad each section.

**Alternative:** Keep them separate but radically shorten Chapter 4. Five constants don't need 2,000 words. Cut to the essential observations and let the chapter breathe.

---

### Chapter 5: A Bestiary of Functions

**Purpose:** Catalog the named special functions (gamma, Fourier, Bessel, etc.) that populate the blog.

**What works:**
- The bestiary metaphor is charming and apt.
- The gamma function section is well-written—explains why it matters, not just how often it appears.
- The "Names Matter" section articulates something true: a name creates a handle.

**What doesn't:**
- Same structural problem as Chapter 4: function after function, each with first/last appearance, each with sample posts. Catalog fatigue.
- The supporting cast (hypergeometric, Legendre) gets cursory treatment—either give them real attention or cut them.
- The chapter ends weakly: "That's what a bestiary is for: not just to catalog, but to enable the hunt." This is portentous without being meaningful.

**Specific suggestion:**
See above—consider merging with Chapter 4. Alternatively, reframe the chapter around *why* these functions matter to programmers and engineers, not just *how often* they appear. The "Names Matter" section gestures at this but doesn't follow through.

---

### Chapter 6: The Mathematicians Behind the Theorems

**Purpose:** Explore how Cook relates to historical mathematicians (Gauss, Euler, Ramanujan, etc.).

**What works:**
- The opening frame (these are "relationships," not citations) is promising.
- The Erdős "God's book" reference is well-chosen.
- The question beneath ("What does it mean to work in a field with a 2,000-year history?") is genuinely interesting.

**What doesn't:**
- The chapter doesn't answer its own question. It catalogs mentions but doesn't interpret them deeply.
- The "Giants" / "Intuitive Genius" / "Connector" / "Bridge Builder" / "Supporting Cast" structure is arbitrary—these labels don't earn their distinctions.
- The Atiyah interview content belongs in Chapter 10 (Interviews), not here.
- The ending ("That's how mathematics works") is flat.

**Specific suggestion:**
This is the weakest chapter. It needs radical reframing or cutting. The interesting question—how does a modern mathematician position himself relative to historical giants—gets lost in tallying. Either commit to that question (which means less about mention counts, more about what the mentions reveal) or cut the chapter and distribute its strongest observations elsewhere.

---

### Chapter 7: Languages as Thinking Tools

**Purpose:** Explore the programming languages (Python, Mathematica, PowerShell) that appear in the blog.

**What works:**
- The opening ("The blog's second audience isn't mathematicians—it's programmers") is a strong reframe.
- The PowerShell "fossil" observation is excellent—a complete arc visible in the data.
- The thesis ("Languages are thinking tools because they shape what's easy to express") is worth stating.

**What doesn't:**
- The Python section is too long for what it says. "Python is the dominant language" doesn't need 500 words.
- The SciPy and Mathematica sections are catalog-mode again.
- The "Translation Layer" section is the most interesting part but arrives late and gets too little space.

**Specific suggestion:**
Reorder: start with the "Translation Layer" thesis, then use the specific languages as evidence. Currently the chapter presents data first, then interpretation. Flip it.

---

### Chapter 8: Cryptography's Moment

**Purpose:** Show how the blog responded to the 2019 privacy/crypto spike.

**What works:**
- The Hardy reference (number theory as useless) is a strong frame.
- The 2019 spike is genuinely dramatic and well-contextualized.
- The RSA and elliptic curve sections connect math to urgent application.
- The closing ("Hardy was wrong about the safety. But he was right that mathematical beauty matters") is strong.

**What doesn't:**
- The chapter oversells its uniqueness. The "spike" framing is repeated too often—we get it by the second mention.
- The HIPAA section feels like it belongs elsewhere (Chapter 9, perhaps, since it's about professional context).
- The Bitcoin cluster could be trimmed—9 posts don't warrant extensive treatment.

**Specific suggestion:**
Tighten the opening. The Hardy quote in the epigraph is enough setup; the first two paragraphs of the chapter body largely repeat it.

---

### Chapter 9: The Edges of the Map

**Purpose:** Explore the non-mathematical categories (Music, Typography, Clinical Trials, Unicode).

**What works:**
- The "edges reveal the person" frame humanizes the data-portrait.
- The Music section is well-written—shows how music becomes math.
- The Clinical Trials "clean break" in 2018 is mysterious and compelling.
- The closing ("The edges aren't distractions from the center. They're extensions of it") is earned.

**What doesn't:**
- The Typography section is thin—50 posts compressed into 150 words.
- The Geography section feels like an afterthought.
- The "Clinical Trial Mystery" section could be developed further—it's the most human moment in the book.

**Specific suggestion:**
Lean into the Clinical Trials mystery more. This is the only place the book approaches something like biography—a visible career transition. It deserves more space and more speculation. What does the silence tell us?

---

### Chapter 10: The Interviews

**Purpose:** Explore the 22 interview posts and what they reveal about Cook's values.

**What works:**
- The Atiyah interview excerpts are excellent ("When someone tells me a general theorem I say that I want an example that is both simple and significant").
- The observation that interview subjects share characteristics (builders, boundary-crossers, concreteness) is sharp.
- The "Interview as Genre" section articulates why interviews matter differently from essays.

**What doesn't:**
- The chapter is too long for its insight. Listing interview subjects by date and word count is catalog mode.
- The Ghrist and Floehr material is good, but the others (Toomey, Franklin) are thin.
- The chapter doesn't connect back to the book's larger argument. So Cook interviewed people—what does that tell us about sustained intellectual practice?

**Specific suggestion:**
Cut the catalog portions. Focus on 2-3 interviews in depth (Atiyah, Ghrist, one entrepreneur). Let those stand for the whole. The chapter should be about what the interviews *reveal*, not about *who was interviewed*.

### Chapter 11: The Evolution of a Blog

**What works:**

- The "What Didn't Change" section (Tuesday, morning, flat monthly) provides satisfying closure.
- The "Why the Evolution Matters" section earns its synthesis.

**What doesn't:**
- Some repetition from earlier chapters (the math percentage rising was covered in Chapter 2).
- The chapter is slightly too long—could be 20% shorter without loss.

**Specific suggestion:**
This is a strong chapter. Minor trims only. Make sure the synthesis feels like arrival, not summary.

### Chapter 12: What the Numbers Miss

**Purpose:** Acknowledge the limits of quantification; honor what can't be counted.

**What works:**

**What doesn't:**

- The chapter could be slightly shorter; some points are made twice.

**Original:**

The internet of 2008 was a different place. People still had blogrolls. Google Reader still existed. The idea that an individual could build something meaningful by simply showing up and writing—no algorithm, no growth hacks, no content strategy—was not yet quaint.

**Suggested:**
> The blog launched in January 2008—the same month Apple announced the iPhone SDK, the same year Twitter was still asking "what are you doing?" People had blogrolls. Google Reader existed. The idea that you could build something meaningful by showing up and writing—no algorithm, no growth hacks, no content strategy—was not yet quaint.

**Why:** The original is slightly overwritten. "The internet of 2008 was a different place" is unnecessary—the details show it. "By simply showing up" can lose "simply." "Not yet quaint" is strong; let it land.

## Title / Subtitle / Hook Suggestions

The current title and subtitle work well:

**Methodical: One Blog, Seventeen Years, 1.8 Million Words**
*A Data Portrait of John D. Cook's Blog*

"Methodical" is both descriptive (the approach) and characterizing (the subject). The subtitle is clear and specific.

Alternative options to consider:

1. **Methodical: A Data Portrait of Seventeen Years** — shorter, lets the "data portrait" concept lead
2. **Methodical: The Shape of Sustained Practice** — emphasizes the transferable insight
3. **Methodical: What One Million Words Looks Like** — more colloquial hook
4. **The Quiet Institution: Seventeen Years of Writing in Public** — elevates the prologue's concept

My recommendation: Keep the current title/subtitle. They're clear, honest, and appropriate for a gift-book.

2. Clarify the "transformation" for non-Cook readers: this book offers permission and method for sustained, quiet work.

4. **Rewrite Chapter 6** to focus on the relationship question rather than tallying mentions, or cut it.

6. **Cut the catalog portions of Chapter 10**—focus on 2-3 interviews in depth.

9. **Reorder Chapter 7** to lead with thesis (languages as thinking tools), then evidence.
10. **Add a "Start Here: 10 Essential Posts"** section to Appendix B.

### Line-Level
11. Trim parenthetical explanations throughout—trust the reader more.
12. Vary chapter endings—too many restate rather than arrive.
13. Find 3-4 moments for wonder, humor, or affection—the voice is slightly austere.
14. Check all pull quotes for relevance—several feel disconnected from their chapters.

## Final Note

This is a strong first draft of an unusual book. The concept works. The voice is confident. The structure is mostly sound, with sag in Part II. The prologue and Chapter 12 are the manuscript's high points—protect them.

The main revision task is compression and variation: make the middle chapters feel less like catalogs, and give readers more reason to keep turning pages when the data tables arrive.

The book knows what it is. The revision is about helping it become that more fully.

---

## Key Takeaways

**Strengths:** The prologue, Chapter 12, and the overall concept are strong. The voice is confident and appropriate. The three-era framework works.

**Primary concern:** Part II (Chapters 4-6) sags into repetitive catalog mode. Consider merging Chapters 4 & 5, and substantially reworking or cutting Chapter 6.

**Structural fixes:** Move the HIPAA material to Chapter 9, cut catalog portions of Chapter 10, move data-dump sections from Chapter 12 to Appendix A.

**The tertiary reader:** The book needs 1-2 paragraphs early on that answer "why should I care if I don't know this blog?"

The closing line of Chapter 12 ("The rest belongs to the readers who found what they needed, exactly when they needed it, and never thought to say thanks") is the book's best sentence. Build toward it.
